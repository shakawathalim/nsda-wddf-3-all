Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

IPM combines several low-risk methods to keep pests below a level that harms your crop. Pesticides are used only when other steps are not enough.

Prevent

Use healthy seed, resistant varieties, crop rotation and clean fields.

Monitor

Scout every week and record what you find, where and when.

Act when needed

Treat when pest numbers pass the level your extension officer advises.

Review

Check results, note what worked, and improve next season.

Prevention methods

The cheapest pest control is the one you never need.

  • Plant certified, disease-free seed and healthy seedlings, and raise nurseries under fine net.
  • Choose varieties with resistance or tolerance to local pests and diseases.
  • Rotate crops. Do not grow the same crop family in the same field every season.
  • Remove crop residue, weeds and fallen fruit that shelter pests.
  • Use balanced fertiliser. Too much nitrogen attracts sap-sucking insects such as planthoppers and aphids.
  • Give plants enough space for air movement, and avoid wetting leaves late in the day.
  • Clean tools and sprayers when moving between fields.

Biological controls

Use living allies to keep pests down.

Natural enemies

Ladybird beetles and lacewings eat aphids. Spiders, dragonflies and predatory bugs eat many pests. Avoid broad-spectrum sprays that kill them.

Parasitic wasps

Tiny Trichogramma wasps lay eggs inside pest eggs, such as stem borer eggs. They are released as egg cards where available through extension services.

Microbial products

Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) controls young caterpillars. Trichoderma helps protect roots from soil-borne diseases.

Bird perches

In rice fields, placing bamboo perches lets birds sit and eat caterpillars and other insects.

Flowering strips

Growing flowers on field edges feeds beneficial insects and keeps them nearby.

Ducks and fish

In some rice-duck or rice-fish systems, ducks and fish eat pests and weeds. Ask local extension if this suits your field.

Organic remedies

Gentler options for small pest problems. Test on a few plants first.

  • Neem: neem seed kernel extract or neem oil deters and slows many insects. Spray in the evening.
  • Soap spray: a mild soap solution controls aphids, whiteflies and mites on contact.
  • Garlic and chilli extract: a home-made spray that repels some soft-bodied insects.
  • Traps: yellow sticky traps for whiteflies and aphids, and pheromone traps for borers and fruit flies.
  • Hand-picking: remove egg masses, caterpillars and infested shoots, and destroy them.
  • Wood ash: dusted lightly on leaves can discourage some crawling insects.

Monitoring pests

  • Walk the field once a week, in a zig-zag pattern.
  • Check 10-20 plants in different spots, including leaf undersides.
  • Count pests and natural enemies, and write the numbers down.
  • Use traps to track when pests arrive.
  • Compare with the action level given by your extension officer.

Early detection

  • Dead hearts or white heads in rice point to stem borer.
  • Curled, sticky or yellowing leaves can mean aphids or whiteflies.
  • Holes, ragged leaves or droppings (frass) show caterpillars.
  • Dark, water-soaked spots on leaves in cool, humid weather suggest blight.
  • Wilting shoots on brinjal often mean borer inside the stem.

Effective treatment

  • Identify the problem first. Send a photo to an expert if unsure.
  • Start with the mildest effective method, and treat only affected areas.
  • Spray in the evening or early morning, and protect bees and pollinators.
  • Follow the product label on dose, protective equipment and days before harvest.
  • Rotate products with different modes of action to slow resistance.
Safety: pesticides can harm people, animals and water. Wear gloves, a mask and long sleeves, never spray against the wind, keep products in original containers away from children, and ask your Upazila agriculture office before using any chemical.
